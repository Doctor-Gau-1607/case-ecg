ECG Blog #553 — What happened?

The ECG in Figure-1 was obtained from an older woman — who presented with a 2-hour history of new-onset severe CP (Chest Pain). Her CP then lessened by the time she was seen.


QUESTIONS:
  • Obviously this is a STEMI. That said — What ELSE would you say about this tracing?
    • What is the probable “culprit” artery?


Figure-1: The initial ECG in today's case — obtained from an older woman with new-onset severe CP. (To improve visualization — I've digitized the original ECG using PMcardio).


My Thoughts on the ECG in Figure-1 …
That this patient is in the midst of an extensive acutely evolving STEMI — is obvious from across the room. But there are a number of additional findings that should be immediately recognized for optimal management.
  • The rhythm is sinus tachycardia at a rate of ~130/minute, with 1 PVC (Premature Ventricular Contraction) best seen toward the end of the long lead II rhythm strip (See Figure-2 below).

  • PEARL #1: Most of the time with uncomplicated acute infarction — the patient will not manifest sinus tachycardia. As a result — the finding of any degree of sinus tachycardia should immediately suggest that something else is going on.
    • That “something else” could be hypotension (from cardiogenic — or any other form of shock) — or it could be in association with a normal BP and some other acute entity (ie, acute pulmonary disease, acid-base or electrolyte abnormality, anemia, acute GI bleed, etc.).
    • Given how extensive the ST elevation in Figure-1 is — and given how fast this sinus tachycardia is ( = ~130/minute! ) — the risk of lethality in this patient with extensive acute ischemia is huge unless prompt cath with PCI is urgently undertaken.

Marked ST-T wave deviation is seen in 11/12 leads in Figure-1:
  • RED arrows highlight dramatic ST elevation.
  • BLUE arrows highlight reciprocal ST depression
  • Lead aVR shows marked ST depression.
  • In the midst of all this ST-T wave deviation — Lead V1 is remarkable for its absence of ST elevation or depression.

QUESTIONS: 
  • Given that 2 of the 3 inferior leads show reciprocal ST depression (BLUE arrows in leads II and III) — WHY does lead II (which is the 3rd inferior lead) show ST elevation instead of ST depression? 
  • Why is there marked ST depression in lead aVR? As discussed in ECG Blog #400 — Usually with DSI (Diffuse Subendocardial Ischemia) there is ST elevation in lead aVR instead of ST depression.
  • And why is there practically no ST-T wave deviation in lead V1?

Figure-2: I've labeled KEY findings from Figure-1.


PEARL #2: While we clearly do not need answers to the above questions to recognize the need for prompt cath — appreciation of the answers to these questions is insightful for identifying the likely “culprit” artery. 
  • ANSWER: I suspect the reason for some of the unexpected findings in today's initial ECG — is that this patient is presenting with acute occlusion of the LMain (Left Main) coronary artery. 


Acute LMain Coronary Occlusion:
Most patients with acute LMain occlusion do not survive to make it to the hospital. Instead — terminal VFib is the usual result, occurring before medical providers with a defibrillator can get to the patient. For this reason — we rarely see what the ECG looks like when there is acute LMain occlusion (which is why collecting data on these patients is so difficult).
  • Dr. Stephen Smith's ECG Blog provides us with rare insight into the ECG findings that may be encountered with acute LMain occlusion. I refer you to the December 18 2025 post in his blog — in which I've updated Dr. Smith's collection of ECGs obtained from patients presenting with documented acute LMain occlusion. As shown below in Figure-3 — this collection now contains 9 patients.

PEARL #3: When a patient with acute LMain occlusion survives long enough for medical providers to record an ECG — You might see anything! 
  • The reason why we might see anything is simply because we have multiple different vectors of ST-T wave deviation from varied contributions of all major coronary arteries. Some give ST elevation vectors — others give reciprocal ST depression. 
  • In any given patient — it is impossible to predict which of these multiple vectors will dominate with ST elevation not cancelled out by opposing ST depression.
  • For example, in Figure-2 — The probable reason why there is ST elevation rather than reciprocal ST depression in lead II — is that at the time that this ECG was recorded, the vector of ST elevation from acute LCx occlusion predominated over the inferior lead reciprocal ST depression vector expected with acute proximal LAD occlusion.

  • Bottom Line: It is rare that you will encounter a patient with acute LMain occlusion. It is virtually impossible to predict what the ECG will look like in such patients. Lead aVR does not provide the answer (as shown in Figure-3 — Lead aVR may show anything!). But at least the findings in Figure-3 give us an idea of the possibilities.
    • See ECG Blog #374 — for another case in which acute LMain occlusion is suspected, but could not be proven.

Figure-3: Reasons for the varied ECG presentation of acute LMain occlusion — with this summary synthesized from My Comment in the December 18, 2025 post of Dr. Smith's ECG Blog.


The CASE Continues:
By the time today's patient arrived at the hospital — her chest pain had significantly decreased, and her condition was stabilizing.
  • A repeat ECG was obtained (seen in Figure-4).

QUESTION: How would you interpret the repeat ECG? 


Figure-4: Comparison of the repeat ECG with the initial tracing.


The repeat ECG:
The "good news" on seeing the repeat ECG in Figure-4 — is that the patient's CP was less and her condition appeared to be stabilizing.
  • The rhythm is still sinus tachycardia, albeit at a slightly slower rate (down from 130 — to 120/minute in ECG #2).

As to ECG #2 — There is less ST elevation compared to the initial ECG. That said — there has been a change in the distribution of acute ST-T wave changes:
  • Lead V1 now shows ST segment straightening and elevation.
  • Although there is decidedly less ST elevation in the chest leads — there is now ST segment straightening in lead V2, still with ST elevation and a hyperacute appearance to the ST-T waves in leads V2,V3,V4. 
  • Surprisingly, there is now a hint of ST depression in lead V6. 
Impression: The chest leads in ECG #2 now strongly suggest the pattern of precordial Swirl consistent with acute proximal LAD occlusion (See ECG Blog #380 — for review of Precordial Swirl). To emphasize — that the pattern of "Swirl" was not present in ECG #1 in which there was no ST elevation in lead V1, and no hint of ST depression in lead V6.
  • Regarding the limb leads in ECG #2 — there is no longer the marked ST elevation in leads I,II,aVL. Instead — We now see reciprocal ST depression in all 3 inferior leads (leads II,III,aVF).
  • The marked ST depression that had been seen in lead aVR is no longer present.
PEARL #4: I have been repeatedly amazed at how much easier it is to appreciate subtle-but-important differences between serial tracings when you put both ECGs together, and assess the 2 tracings by lead-to-lead comparison. Until I did this — the only thing I appreciated was that ECG #2 looked "better" than ECG #1. 
  • I suspect that today's patient presented with acute LMain occlusion. The subtle changes described above that we see in Figure-4 can best be explained by some amount of spontaneous reperfusion of the LMain coronary artery that must have allowed some perfusion of the LCx (which is why the dramatic ST elevation that we saw in leads I,aVL; V4,V5,V6 in ECG #1 has almost completely resolved).
  • As a result of this restoration of flow in the LCx — the ST-T wave changes of acute proximal LAD occlusion are no longer attenuated by blocked flow in the LCx (which is why we now see the unopposed Precordial Swirl pattern in the chest leads of ECG #2). It is also why we now see reciprocal ST depression in all 3 inferior leads, with slight ST elevation in lead aVL and less ST depression in lead aVR.

================================

Today's CASE Concludes ...
Given this patient's improving clinical condition — the decision was made not to perform emergent cardiac catheterization. Unfortunately — I lack further follow-up of this case beyond knowing that the patient was transferred to a referral facility, and that she was pain-free at the time ECG #3 was recorded (shown as the bottom tracing in Figure-4). I don't know the details of her medical treatment.
 
  • Your thoughts on the decision not to perform emergent cath?

Figure-5: The patient was transferred to a referral center. She was pain-free on arrival at the transfer hospital.


Concluding Thoughts:
I suspect today's patient presented to the hospital with acute LMain occlusion — which then spontaneously reperfused with resolution of her chest pain. In ECG #3 — almost all acute ST-T wave changes have now resolved.
  • Because of clinical improvement — emergent cath was not performed. 
  • The problem with the decision not to perform prompt cath is the clinical reality that acute coronary occlusion is an evolving process. What spontaneously reopens — may just as easily spontaneously reocclude at any point in the process until the final state of infarction is reached. Given how extensive ST-T wave changes were on this patient's initial ECG — reocclusion without preventive stenting may prove fatal.

ECG Blog #555 — Too Irregular for VT?


I was sent the tracing shown in Figure-1 — and asked for my impression about the cardiac rhythm? I was told that the patient was a 70-year old woman — who presented to the ED with confusion and hypotension. She had an abnormal medical history, including "some cardiac issues" — but was not known to have WPW. 


QUESTIONS:
  • What is the rhythm in Figure-1? 
    • How would you treat this patient?
    • Could the rhythm be VT despite its irregularity? 

Figure-1: The initial ECG in today's case — obtained from a 70-year old woman with confusion and hypotension. (To improve visualization — I've digitized the original ECG using PMcardio).


What is the Rhythm in Figure-1?
I favor assessment of the rhythm by use of the P’s, Q’s, 3R Approach ( = “Watch your Ps, Qs and the 3 Rs” — as in ECG Blog #185). 
  • To emphasize — Although I always consider each of the 5 parameters in the Ps,Qs,3Rs — I vary the sequence in which I assess these parameters, depending on which parameter(s) is easiest to assess in the tracing I am looking at.

Applying this approach to the long lead V1 rhythm strip in Figure-1 — I noted the following:
  • The QRS is clearly wide in all 12 leads (This could be VT! ).
  • That said — against VT is that although the rhythm looks to be almost regular in some parts of the tracing — the rhythm in Figure-1 is not Regular. On looking at the entire long lead V1 rhythm strip — we can clearly see variation in the R-R interval.
  • Since the rhythm in Figure-1 is irregular — the Rate will vary (That said, the ventricular rate is obviously fast — as the R-R interval throughout the long lead V1 rhythm strip is most often less than 2 large boxes in duration. This suggests a ventricular rate well over 150/minute).
  • P waves are absent. Therefore, there is no Relation of atrial activity to neighboring QRS complexes.

Impression: Assessment of the Ps,Qs,3Rs in Figure-1 — suggests that the rhythm in today's tracing is an irregular WCT (Wide-Complex Tachycardia) in which P waves are absent. Given QRS widening — the differential diagnosis is between: i) VT (despite the irregularity); — vs — ii) AFib with a rapid ventricular response (given that the rhythm is fast and irregularly irregular without P waves).
  • More than just QRS widening — QRS morphology is strongly suggestive of a ventricular etiology. In addition to marked fragmentation (notching) of the QRS complex (an indication of "scar" — consistent with underlying structural heart disease) — there is an all negative QRS in leads V4,V5,V6 which is distinctly uncommon in supraventricular rhythms (See review of the 3 "Simple Rules" in the Addendum to ECG Blog #283).
  • That said — the irregular irregularity and lack of P waves in Figure-1 is equally suggestive of AFib as the etiology, with QRS widening due to either rate-related aberrant conduction or preexisting bundle branch block.

PEARL #1: Clinically — it does not matter what the etiology of today's rhythm is! This is because this patient is hemodynamically unstable (ie, mentally confused and hypotensive — as noted in the brief history we were given).
  • Determination of hemodynamic "stability" is an art dependent on a series of clinical factors that defy precise criteria, with need for moment-to-moment modification based on "being there" = at the patient's bedside (See the Audio Pearl in ECG Blog #220 — for more on assessing hemodynamic stability).
  • As a result, immediate synchronized cardioversion is the treatment of choice in today's case — regardless of whether the etiology of the rhythm is an irregular VT vs AFib with QRS widening due to either aberrant conduction or preexisting bundle branch block (ie, Synchronized cardioversion is likely to convert many [most] supraventricular rhythms — as well as monomorphic VT).
  • The clinical reality is that we are often called on to begin treatment before we can be certain of the etiology of the rhythm we are treating. In this situation — assessment for hemodynamic stability takes precedence. If the patient is unstable ==> cardiovert. If stable despite the arrhythmia — then we have at least a moment of time to look closer at the rhythm or to empirically treat based on our best guess of the rhythm etiology.
==============================

Today's CASE Continues ...           
Given hemodynamic instability — the patient received prompt electrical cardioversion. This converted the patient's rhythm to the tracing shown in the bottom panel of Figure-2. 

QUESTION:
  • How would YOU interpret the result of synchronized cardioversion that is shown in Figure-2?
  • What does the repeat ECG done after cardioversion tell you about the etiology of ECG #1?
   
Figure-2: Comparison between today's initial ECG — and the repeat ECG recorded after synchronized cardioversion.

============================== 

ANSWER:
The repeat ECG in Figure-2 now shows sinus rhythm! (ie, There are now upright P waves in lead II, with a constant and normal PR interval in front of each QRS complex). This proves that the initial ECG was not VT — because QRS morphology is virtually identical during the irregular WCT and during sinus rhythm. Instead — this confirms a supraventricular etiology for the irregular WCT rhythm that we saw in Figure-1.
  • Given the irregular irregularity of ECG #1, and the lack of P waves — this confirms that today's initial ECG represents AFib with a rapid ventricular reponse.

  • PEARL #2: As rare as it is for a supraventricular rhythm to manifest an all negative QRS complex in lateral chest lead V6 —  today's case illustrates that virtually anything is possible. This should not be so surprising in today's case given how abnormal the baseline tracing ECG #2 truly is (ie, This repeat ECG shows sinus rhythm, RBBB/LAHB — with marked fragmentation in almost every lead — as well as QS waves in leads V4,V5,V6 + inferior Q waves [hard to tell if a tiny initial r wave is or is not present in these inferior leads] ).
  • The "good news" — is that despite how abnormal QRS morphology is in the repeat ECG after cardioversion to sinus rhythm — no acute ST-T wave changes are seen. So whatever scarring (and prior infarctions) that may have occurred — there is no indication of a new acute event.

PEARL #3: As helpful as QRS morphology is for determining the etiology of a WCT rhythm without sinus P waves — QRS morphology is not infallible! Every now-and-again I'll review a case in which despite strong suggestion from grossly abnormal QRS morphology that a WCT rhythm is VT — the patient will turn out to have a baseline tracing during sinus rhythm with an identical bizarre QRS morphology (as was seen in today's case).

==============================    

Can VT be Irregular?

I conclude today's blog post by returning to one of the questions posed at the beginning of today's case: Can VT be irregular?


The answer to the above question: It depends! 

  • Monomorphic VT (ie, in which QRS morphology is constant throughout the arrhythmia) — is usually a regular rhythm once the monomorphic VT has become established. There may be some initial irregularity when the VT is just getting started (“warm-up” period) — as well as some terminal irregularity (“cool-down” period) when the VT is stopping. However, once established, the reentrant circuit of most monomorphic forms of VT tends to be stable, which results in a regular R-R interval.
  • In contrast — PMVT (PolyMorphic VT) by definition is irregular (usually irregularly irregular) — because there is no stable reentrant circuit, and QRS morphology is constantly changing.
  • Rarely seen exceptions to the above generalities are noted in ECG Blog #231, which reviews the 4 principal forms of VT. In addition to PMVT — established VT will be irregular with BiDirectional VT and Pleomorphic VT (in which there may be more than a single VT circuit — therefore producing variation in VT morphology). To emphasize that both bidirectional and pleomorphic VT are usually easy to distinguish from monomorphic VT because of the variation in VT morphology.
  • An exception to the above generalities in which VT with a single QRS morphology is irregular — may occur if there is Wenckebach Exit Block out of the VT focus (in which VT morphology is constant — but there is a pattern to the R-R interval, resulting in 2:1, 3:2 and/or some other ratio of conduction of VT impulses out of the ectopic impulse site). That said, despite looking over a period of decades for examples of VT with Wenckebach exit block — I’ve only seen a handful of cases.

PEARL #4: The final form of VT that may manifest a single QRS morphology despite unpredictable variability in the R-R interval — is focal “triggered” activity. The mechanism of focal "triggered" activity is complex (Lerman et al — JACC 10(2):379-401, 2024) — but is thought to relate to abnormal membrane voltage oscillations (afterdepolarizations) that may sometimes attain the threshold needed to fire a new ectopic impulse. If this action repeats — the result may be an irregular monomorpic VT.
  • Take-Home Point: Not all VT with a single QRS morphology will be regular. Failure to appreciate this point may lead to mistaking “triggered” VT for AFib with QRS morphology widened either by preexisting bundle branch block or rate-related aberrant conduction (Zhang et al — Circulation 139(14):1750, 2019).

P.S.: As noted above — today's rhythm represents AFib with a rapid ventricular response. The question arises as to whether QRS widening in this case could be the result of WPW, with QRS widening due to conduction over an AP (Accessory Pathway)?
  • Although impossible to rule out WPW on the basis of the single ECG shown in Figure-1 — this seems unlikely since: i) The patient has no history of WPW-associated arrhythmias — and it would be highly unusual for a patient to suddenly manifest AP conduction at age 70! — and, ii) Although the rate of this patient's AFib is fast — it is usually even faster when a patient with WPW suddenly develops AFib (ie, usually >220/minute in places, with occasional short R-R intervals consistent with rates ≥250/minute).

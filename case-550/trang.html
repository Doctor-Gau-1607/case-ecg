ECG Blog #550 — Guess the Clinical Setting

The ECG in Figure-1 was obtained from a middle-aged man who presented with CP (Chest Pain). I was asked for my thoughts on the rhythm.


QUESTIONS:
  • What is the rhythm?
    • How certain are you of your answer?
    • What is the probable cause of this rhythm?

Figure-1: The initial ECG in today's case. (To improve visualization — I've digitized the original ECG using PMcardio).



My Approach to Today's Tracing: 
We are at a distinct disadvantage in Figure-1 for determining the rhythm. This is because we only see 9 beats, and because we lack a long lead rhythm strip. Nevertheless, a number of deductions can confidently be made.
  • PEARL #1: LIfe is easier — IF we simply number the beats (as I have done in Figure-2). Doing so allows us to talk intelligently with colleagues about what is going on (as well as making it easier for us to appreciate relationships between P waves and neighboring QRS complexes).

Take Another Look in Figure-2:
  • HINT:  Look first at the chest leads ...

Figure-2: I've numbered the beats in today's tracing.

==================================


What We See in Figure-2:
P waves are present in front of beats 7,8,9 (RED arrows in Figure-3):
  • The P wave in front of beat #7 is clearly too short to be normally conducted to the ventricles. Thus, at least for this one beat #7 — there is AV dissociation (which simply means that the P wave in front of beat #7 is not completely conducting this QRS complex).

Figure-3: RED arrows highlight the presence of sinus P waves.


PEARL #2: Recognizing that the PR interval in front of beat #7 is too short to be a normal sinus-conducted beat is the KEY clue to today's rhythm — as it tells us that beat #7 must be a ventricular escape beat!
  • In contrast — the QRS complex in front of beats #8 and 9 is narrow, very different-looking than the QRS of beats #7,8,9 — and, the PR interval in front of beats #8 and 9 is constant and long enough to be sinus-conducted (I measure the PR interval in front of beats #7,8 to be ≥0.13-0.14 — and the PR interval might be longer if we were to see P waves in lead II).
  • This tells us that beats #5 and 6 must be ventricular escape beats at the slightly accelerated rate of ~55/minute (the R-R interval between beats #5-6 and 6-7 being between 5-to-6 large boxes). 

  • PEARL #3: If we focus our attention on lead V3 — Doesn't the QRST complex of beat 7 look slightly different than the QRST of beats #5 and 6? (At least in lead V3 — the QRS and T wave of beat #7 are a little less tall than for beats #5 and 6). This tells us that beat #7 is a fusion beat, and proves that beats #5 and 6 are ventricular in origin (See ECG Blog #128 — for more on fusion beats).
  • NOTE: It makes sense that beat #7 is a fusion beat — because the PR interval in front of this beat is just a little bit shorter than the PR interval of the sinus-conducting P waves in front of beats #8 and 9 (as we'll see momentarily in the Laddergram in Figure-5).

PEARL #4: That beats #5 and 6 are ventricular escape beats — is supported by another look at QRST morphology of beats #1-thru-4 in the limb leads:
  • Note in lead I — that the QRS complex begins with a very deep and wide Q wave! We virtually never see such a deep and wide initial negative deflection in left-sided lead I with normal sinus conduction. Thus, this initial deep and wide Q wave in lead I strongly suggests a ventricular etiology for the 4 beats in this lead.
  • PEARL #5: Note the subtle-but-real negative deflection at the end of the QRS in each of the 3 inferior leads (YELLOW arrows in Figure-4 highlighting this negative deflection in lead III — but with a similar terminal negative deflection also being seen in leads II and aVF). This terminal negative notch most likely reflects retrograde conduction of the P wave from ventricular beats #1,2,3,4.
  • I suspect retrograde conduction continues in the chest leads, and is seen after beats #5 and 6 — until slight acceleration of the underlying sinus P wave rhythm allows the RED arrow P waves in front of beats #8 and 9 to recapture the ventricles with sinus-conducted beats #8 and 9 (See the laddergram below in Figure-5). 

Figure-4: YELLOW arrows suggest retrograde conduction of P waves following ventricular escape beats.



Figure-5: Laddergram suggesting the mechanism of today’s rhythm.


My Impression: I suspect the rhythm in Figure-5 begins with 7 beats of AIVR (Accelerated IdioVentricular Rhythm) at ~55-60/minute — and ends with 2 sinus-capture beats ( = beats #8 and 9).
  • The first 6 ventricular beats manifest retrograde P waves (dotted RED lines in the laddergram that are headed back to the atria).
  • Slight acceleration of the rate of sinus impulses allows the RED arrow P waves to recapture the ventricles with beats #8 and 9.
  • The overly short PR interval and intermediate shape of the QRS complex and of the T wave for beat #7 — suggests this is a fusion beat. Early arrival of this 1st RED arrow P wave in the ventricles prevents retrograde conduction of ventricular beat #7 — which therefore makes it possible for the 2nd RED arrow P wave to “capture” the ventricles with a sinus-conducted beat.
==================================

Putting It All Together:
  • Today’s patient presented with CP.
  • The underlying rhythm in the initial ECG appears to be sinus bradycardia and arrhythmia that is intermittently interrupted by AIVR at a rate of ~55-60/minute.

  • PEARL #6: The combination of AIVR in a patient who presents to the ED for CP — is commonly seen during the evolution of an acute OMI ( = Occlusion-based Myocardial Infarction). As a result — We need to go back and Take Another LOOK at the initial ECG in today's case, looking to see if there might be subtle ECG clues of a recent OMI (keeping in mind that acute ST-T wave changes of an OMI may be difficult to recognize in the presence of a ventricular rhythm such as AIVR).

PEARL #7: When AIVR is seen in association with an evolving OMI (as is especially likely when the patient presents with CP, as in today's case) — it often represents a reperfusion arrhythmia (See ECG Blog #108 — for the basics regarding AIVR).
  • As a result — correlating each serial ECG with the presence and severity of CP becomes especially important for understanding the sequence of events (and for determining whether the "culprit" artery is likely to be open or closed at the time a given ECG is recorded). 
  • In today's case — We would want to find out IF this patient's CP had decreased (or resolved) at the time the initial ECG was recorded. If symptoms are less at the time of this initial ECG before any treatment has been administered — this would suggest that there has been spontaneous reperfusion of the "culprit" artery (in which case ECG signs of acute infarction may be especially subtle!).

In Figure-6 — I've reproduced today's initial ECG.
  • Take another LOOK at this initial ECG — with special attention looking for subtle signs of recent infarction.

Figure-6: I've reproduced today's initial ECG.

==================================

Subtle ECG Signs of Recent Infarction:
Despite the presence of AIVR (and the likelihood of sponaneous reperfusion) — I thought there are subtle ECG signs of recent infarction in today's initial ECG (See labeled Figure-7 below):
  • I focused first on the 2 sinus-conducted beats — since assessment of ST-T waves for acute changes is more reliable when seen in normally conducted beats than it is when looking at ventricular beats ( = beats #8 and 9 — within the RED rectangle in Figure-7).
  • The amount of ST elevation in lead V2 for beats #8,9 looks to be disproportionately increased given modest depth of the S wave in these sinus-conducted beats.
  • The ST segment takeoff in lead V3 looks to be straightened for sinus-conducted beats #8,9.
  • There is ST segment straightening with slight depression in lateral chest lead V6 (BLUE arrow in this lead).   
To Emphasize: Acute ST-T wave changes can sometimes be seen in ventricular beats. It's just that these changes tend to be subtle and more difficult to recognize than when acute changes are seen in normally conducted beats:
  • In the limb leads (which show 4 consecutive ventricular beats) — the overly "bulky" and depressed ST-T waves in each of the inferior leads looks disproportionate and "off" for what I'd expect given modest QRS amplitude in leads II,III,aVF (RED arrows in these leads).
  • Given this inferior lead ST-T wave appearance — the elevated and overly large upright T waves in leads I and aVL manifest the mirror-image opposite picture of what we see in the inferior leads (BLUE arrows in leads I and aVL).
  • In the chest leads — the ST-T wave in lead V2 looks to be disproportionately large, with an angled and overly elevated J-point for the ventricular beats. Especially in leads V4,V5,V6 — the T waves look disproportionately large given the tiny size of the QRS in these lateral chest leads (RED arrows in the above noted chest leads for ventricular beats #5,6,7).
Impression: The above noted subtle signs in multiple leads of Figure-7 in this patient with CP and intermittent AIVR are suggestive of a Precordial "Swirl" pattern from recent acute LAD occlusion (See ECG Blog #380 — for review of Precordial Swirl).
  • To Emphasize: The above ECG findings are subtle! But especially IF this patient's CP was less (or resolved) at the time the ECG in Figure-7 was recorded — we are expecting less obvious ECG signs than what would probably have been seen if severe CP was still present.

Figure-7: I've labeled a series of subtle ST-T wave abnormalities present in Figure-6 that suggest there has been a recent OMI.

==================================

The CASE Concludes:
Providers were appropriately concerned by this patient's history of new CP in association in association with today's initial ECG.
  • Cardiac cath was performed in a timely manner — and revealed acute proximal LAD occlusion, amenable to PCI. 
  • A repeat ECG done the next day after successful PCI is shown below in Figure-8.

QUESTIONS:
  • How do you interpret the repeat ECG in Figure-8?
  • How do beats #8 and 9 in ECG #2 compare with beats #8 and 9 in Figure-7?
  • Why might the voltage be so low in Figure-8?

Figure-8: Repeat ECG done the day after successful PCI.

==================================

ANSWERS:
AIVR has resolved — with reestablishment of a sinus rhythm in the repeat ECG recorded after successful PCI (RED arrows showing sinus bradycardia at ~55/minute throughout the tracing in Figure-9).
  • Note that all 5 sinus-conducted beats in the chest leads of this repeat ECG look identical to sinus-conducted beats #8 and 9 in today's initial ECG.
  • Note that the QRS in Figure-9 is now narrow in all beats — whereas the QRS was wide for the ventricular beats in Figure-6. The wide initial Q wave in lead I that tipped us off in the initial ECG to the presence of AIVR is no longer present.
  • There is poor R wave progression in the chest leads of Figure-6 (a QS is present in leads V1,V2 — and transition to a predominant R wave never occurs). This is consistent with this patient's recent anterior MI.
  • This post-PCI tracing is also remarkable for diffuse low voltage (No R wave exceeding 5 mm in the limb leads — with overall reduced QRS amplitude also in the chest leads). As highlighted in other cases on this ECG Blog — the finding of low voltage in a patient with recent infarction may be a sign of myocardial "stunning", with reduced cardiac output (See ECG Blog #272 — for more on the causes of low voltage).

Figure-9: I've labeled sinus P waves in the repeat ECG.


Final Thoughts:  
Common things are common. Recognition of AIVR in a patient who presents with CP is an important clue to the likelihood of recent infarction. This should prompt:
  • Attention to the details of the patient's history — with efforts to closely correlate the presence and relative severity of CP to the timing of each ECG. Recognition of AIVR at a time when the patient's CP has decreased or resolved is an important clue that spontaneous reperfusion may have occurred.
  • ECG signs of recent infarction may be especially subtle following spontaneous reperfusion — as was probably the case for today's initial ECG.
  • The ECG signs of recent infarction in Figure-7 are admittedly subtle! That said — these subtle signs become much easier to recognize when you know that the combination of CP and AIVR is commonly seen following recent infarction (and when you know that ECG clues may be very subtle if the patient's CP has decreased or resolved at the time the initial ECG was recorded).
